The company had to run its IT, its cloud services and its first AI systems securely, and at the same time show that it met the relevant requirements from NIS2, TISAX, ISO 27001, GDPR, KRITIS and sector-specific rules. That work needed technical leadership on a part-time basis. We filled the virtual CTO role for several years and built a **security and compliance operating model** that ties strategy, policies, the risk process, identities, incident response and awareness into one structure.

The model enabled the management team, IT operations, engineering and the business functions to make security decisions themselves, to back requirements with evidence, and to adopt new technology without slowing the business down. Security became part of normal decision-making instead of an annual audit exercise.

"Security and compliance operating model" describes the structure that emerged during that time. It is not an official product name of the company.

## Starting point

The requirements arrived from several directions at once. Applications ran in the cloud, automation and integrations needed their own credentials, and the first AI features went into operation. On top of that came obligations from NIS2, TISAX, ISO 2700x, GDPR, KRITIS and sector-specific rules that had to be not only implemented but evidenced: which systems exist, which risks they carry, who or what has access to them, and how the company responds to an incident.

That work needs a role that owns it continuously, makes technical decisions, and carries them up to management. That was the job of the virtual CTO: bring strategy, engineering and evidence together without adding another layer of hierarchy.

## What we built

The result was an operating model with seven parts that reference each other:

- a security strategy and policies that translate into concrete requirements for systems and processes;
- an ISMS with clear roles, a risk register and documentation that describes the current state;
- a connection to the existing SOC, so threat reports are assessed and turned into action;
- identity and access management that covers machine identities as well;
- incident response processes for detection, containment and recovery;
- a training and awareness programme for all staff;
- rules for AI usage and shadow IT that make new tools reviewable instead of forbidding them.

Different roles used the model in different ways. Management decided on investments and residual risks on that basis, IT operations implemented technical controls, engineering needed binding rules for interfaces and access, and business functions requested new tools and AI services through it. The existing SOC supplied the threat analysis, and the outside connections ran to authorities, industry bodies and service providers.

## How a requirement became an action and a piece of evidence

To keep the model from becoming paperwork, every security case ran through the same sequence:

1. Capture the requirement – a new regulatory obligation, a SOC alert, an AI initiative from a business function, or a vulnerability found in operations.
2. Assess the risk – affected systems and data, likelihood, impact on operations, existing controls.
3. Prioritise and decide – a recommendation with residual risk, effort and an owner, written so that management could decide on it without security jargon.
4. Implement – a policy, a technical control, an access restriction, or a change to a process.
5. Record the evidence – decision, implementation and effectiveness, in a form that held up in an audit.
6. Follow up – check effectiveness, update the risk register, adjust the model.

This sequence was the core of the model. It turned scattered security measures into a repeatable process in which every decision had an owner, a date and a piece of evidence.

## ISMS, risk work and the SOC

The ISMS was the working basis: an inventory of systems and data, a risk register with assessments and measures, roles and responsibilities, policies for operations, and a process to keep all of it current. We aligned the structure with the requirements of ISO/IEC 27001, the standard for information security management systems ([ISO/IEC 27001:2022](https://www.iso.org/standard/27001)). The benefit was not the certification logic but the order: every control hung off a risk, and every risk had an owner.

Cyber intelligence did not become a separate data collection; it fed into the risk process. The established SOC delivered threat analyses and attack patterns, we defined the interface, prioritised what was relevant to the company's own systems, and derived measures from it. Risks were assessed by impact on operations, not by the volume of the alert.

## Identities and Zero Trust, including machines

Access rights were where technical security and daily work met most directly. We therefore ran identity and access management as its own workstream: roles and permissions, granting and revoking access, reviewing existing rights, logging access.

A second, often overlooked part was machine identities. Service accounts, API keys, credentials in CI/CD pipelines and connections between cloud services are not people, but they need the same discipline: named owners, limited rights, regular rotation and a clear way to remove them again. These accounts otherwise stay valid and unnoticed for years.

The guiding principle was Zero Trust as the NIST describes it in SP 800-207: trust does not follow from network location but from evaluating every request against identity, device and context ([NIST SP 800-207](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/207/final)). In practice that meant fewer blanket network access rules, tighter permissions per application, and more attention to the question of who or what actually needs an account.

## Incident response: detect, contain, recover

For incidents we defined a process that does not start in the middle of an emergency: detection and classification, roles and escalation paths, technical containment, communication inside and outside the company, recovery of operations, and a review with concrete changes. We led the process and fed the lessons from each case back into the ISMS, the risk register and the awareness work.

The important part was separating responsibility from urgency: who decides during an incident, who intervenes technically, who speaks to customers and authorities, and who documents afterwards. Those roles were defined in advance so they did not have to be negotiated when it mattered.

## Compliance and audit readiness

Requirements from several frameworks overlap and in places contradict each other. We therefore kept a requirements catalogue that mapped each obligation to a concrete control and a piece of evidence: NIS2 as the European cybersecurity directive with its risk management and reporting duties ([Directive (EU) 2022/2555](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022L2555)), TISAX as an assessment and exchange scheme for information security in supply chains ([TISAX](https://www.enx.com/en-US/tisax/)), the GDPR requirements for handling personal data ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)), the specific duties of critical infrastructure operators ([BSI: KRITIS](https://www.bsi.bund.de/EN/Themen/Regulierte-Wirtschaft/kritis-und-regulierte-unternehmen_node.html)), and any sector-specific rules that applied on top.

Audit preparation was a permanent part of the role rather than a project at year end. The documentation was built so that evidence came out of daily operations: decisions with their reasoning, implemented controls with dates, reviewed access rights and training records. Before an assessment the work was then about the completeness of the files, not about whether the measures existed at all.

## Protecting in-house AI systems and shadow IT

AI features went into operation and the matching rules had to be created alongside them. The risks are well documented, starting with prompt injection: inputs that change a model's behaviour in unintended ways and can carry hidden instructions from third-party content ([OWASP LLM01: Prompt Injection](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)). Next to it stood the leakage of data through prompts, responses and logs, and tools that business functions procured without involving IT.

We turned that into review rules: which data may go into a model, which rights an agent receives, what is logged, who approves a tool and how it is switched off again. New AI initiatives ran through the same sequence as any other requirement – risk assessment, decision, evidence. That left room to experiment without letting data flows grow uncontrolled.

## Management, awareness and external partners

Security work rarely fails on technology; it fails on decisions. For management we translated risk into business language: what an outage means for customers and revenue, which residual risk remains after a control, which investment changes what. On that basis the management team could decide instead of handing security questions back to IT.

Awareness was the second lever. A training programme covered security basics and the use of AI tools, supported by clear rules for access and data. Alongside it came cooperation with authorities and industry bodies on requirements and reporting, plus the management of external service providers, whose access and obligations we placed in the same model.

Balancing security and agility was a permanent task. Not every requirement was answered with a ban; often the better answer was a tighter technical control that let the business process continue.

## Outcome

After several years in the role, the company retained an operating model that was embedded in its own organisation:

- a security strategy and policies that were applied in day-to-day operations;
- a maintained ISMS with a risk register, defined roles and documented decisions;
- identity and access management covering people and machine identities;
- incident response processes with defined roles and escalation paths;
- a requirements catalogue with evidence that prepared assessments;
- a training programme and rules for AI usage and shadow IT;
- decision papers that let management assess security risk themselves.

The model put security work into the company's normal workflows. Requirements became actions, actions became evidence, and responsibility stayed where it belongs: with the people who run the systems.
