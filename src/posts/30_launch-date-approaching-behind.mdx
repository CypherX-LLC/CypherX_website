A launch date creates focus, but it can also hide risk. When the date is approaching and the product is behind, teams often respond by adding scope, extending hours, and reporting optimistic percentages. None of those actions answers the critical question: what can be released safely, and when?

If you are thinking, **“Our launch date is approaching and we are behind,”** start with delivery triage.

## Replace activity with evidence

A backlog full of completed tasks can still produce an unready product. Before changing the plan, review the evidence:

- Which core user journeys work from start to finish?
- What defects can block customers or revenue?
- Which integrations and environments are reliable?
- Can the team deploy and roll back safely?
- Is monitoring ready to detect important failures?
- What work is essential for the first release?

This separates launch-critical risk from work that can follow after launch.

## Define the smallest credible release

A late product needs a sharper boundary. Identify the minimum release that delivers the intended business outcome without hiding unacceptable risk.

That may mean reducing scope, sequencing features, using a controlled rollout, or postponing nonessential polish. It does not mean ignoring quality. A smaller release with known limits is easier to support than a larger release that the team cannot observe or recover.

The definition of “ready” should be explicit. Stakeholders, product, engineering, and operations need to agree on the conditions that must be true before launch.

## Triage the delivery system too

Schedule pressure often exposes process problems that existed earlier: slow reviews, unclear ownership, flaky tests, manual deployment, or too many priorities competing for the same people.

A short delivery review can reveal where time is being lost and which intervention has the greatest effect. The answer may be technical, organizational, or both.

## Use a short pilot to create momentum

When the launch is close, a focused pilot completed in a few weeks can target one critical blocker: release readiness, deployment reliability, test coverage around a core workflow, or a delivery bottleneck.

The pilot creates evidence quickly and gives both sides a chance to evaluate how the work gets done. If the collaboration improves the launch path, the engagement can continue with a more informed scope.

## What to do this week

- Freeze nonessential scope changes.
- Identify the critical user journeys.
- Rank issues by customer and business impact.
- Name owners for every launch blocker.
- Test the deployment and rollback path.
- Establish a short, honest status rhythm.
- Decide what happens if the date must move.

## Final thoughts

Being behind schedule is not the same as being out of options. A disciplined triage process can turn panic into decisions: what to ship, what to defer, what to fix, and what evidence is still missing.
