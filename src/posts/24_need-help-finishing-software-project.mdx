When a software project is almost finished but not actually shippable, the pressure is unusually high. The team has already spent the budget, stakeholders expect a result, and the remaining work is often the least visible: unresolved defects, unclear ownership, brittle deployment steps, or decisions nobody wants to make.

If you are thinking, **“We need help finishing this project,”** the first step is not to add more people blindly. It is to create a clear recovery plan.

## Why late-stage projects become difficult

Projects rarely fall behind because of one missing feature. More often, several small issues compound:

- The definition of “done” is unclear.
- Critical work is mixed with nice-to-have work.
- Bugs and technical debt are competing with launch commitments.
- Nobody has a reliable view of what is deployable.
- The original team is exhausted or has moved to other priorities.
- Stakeholders receive activity updates instead of evidence of progress.

Adding another developer to that environment can increase coordination costs. Before implementation resumes, someone needs to understand the current state and make the path to delivery visible.

## Start with a project recovery assessment

A useful recovery assessment should answer five practical questions:

1. What can be shipped today?
2. What prevents a safe release?
3. Which open items are essential to the business outcome?
4. Which decisions are blocked by missing information or ownership?
5. What is the shortest credible path to completion?

This is not a request for a lengthy report. The goal is to turn a confusing backlog into a prioritized plan with owners, dependencies, and risks.

A technical review can also expose issues that are easy to miss under deadline pressure: missing tests, unstable environments, undocumented deployment steps, or a feature that works in a demo but fails under realistic use.

## A small first step reduces risk

When a project is already in trouble, a large new commitment can feel risky. A focused pilot gives everyone a better way to evaluate the partnership. Within a few weeks, the team can assess the current state, address a meaningful blocker, and decide whether continued support is justified.

That approach gives you a chance to evaluate how the partner communicates, handles uncertainty, and delivers under pressure before expanding the engagement.

## What to prepare before asking for help

You do not need perfect documentation. Gather what you have:

- Repository and environment access
- Current backlog or issue tracker
- Latest roadmap or launch expectation
- Known production issues
- Deployment instructions
- Relevant stakeholder and team contacts

Be candid about what is unknown. Honest context helps a recovery team move faster than an optimistic status report.

## Final thoughts

Finishing a delayed project is not just a matter of working harder. It requires separating essential work from noise, finding the real blockers, and creating a delivery plan everyone can trust.
